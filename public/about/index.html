About

When something does not make sense, ask why.

October 2013. Off Italy.

A ship that never existed sailed the word “pwned”.

Researchers at Trend Micro built it with a 700-euro AIS radio and a laptop near a port, and public tracking maps showed it. In the same tests a real tugboat left the Mississippi and turned up on a lake in Dallas. Nobody had to break into anything. AIS simply believes what it hears.

MIT Technology Review, 18 October 2013

Why we exist

Thirteen years later the trick is ordinary. Ships near Istanbul report themselves in Peru. Ships in Shanghai appear in rings on dry land. Whole sea areas lose their satellite signal. Each of those false positions ends up in a port log, an insurance file, a sanctions screen or a court bundle.

Machines now report where they are all the time: ships, aircraft, vehicles, sensors. Almost nobody checks them independently. That is the job we picked.

Question. Investigate. Verify.

Question. Start with the report that does not fit: the impossible speed, the ship on land, the crowd in one spot.

Investigate. Test it against what the physical world allows, compare independent sources, and measure how unusual the pattern really is.

Verify. Write down what the evidence supports and where it stops, and hand over the data so anyone can rerun it. When we are wrong, the correction goes where the claim was.

The name

Bentham’s panopticon is a prison with a tower in the middle: the guard sees every cell and no prisoner sees the guard. Take the “pan” away and you have Anopticon. We kept the tower and took out the secret.

Say it an-OP-ti-kon. The word is older than us; a 2009 project in Venice used it to map the city’s CCTV cameras. We are the maritime one, founded in 2026 in Gujarat.

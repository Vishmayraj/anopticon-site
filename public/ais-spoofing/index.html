AIS spoofing

How a ship comes to broadcast a position it is not in, and how to tell.

A radio beacon on every ship.

The Automatic Identification System makes every large ship broadcast its name, position, course and speed every few seconds on two VHF channels anyone can receive. Under the SOLAS convention it is carried by ships of 300 gross tonnage and upwards on international voyages, by cargo ships of 500 gross tonnage and upwards, and by all passenger ships.

The position in each message comes from the ship’s own satellite-navigation receiver. AIS repeats whatever that receiver believes.

Three ways the beacon lies.

Jamming
Noise drowns the satellite signal. The receiver loses its fix; positions freeze, drift or vanish.
Spoofing
A false signal replaces the real one. The receiver reports the wrong place with full confidence, often many ships at once, often on land.
Forgery
The messages themselves are altered: a borrowed identity, a transponder switched off, a track that was never sailed.

How you tell.

The hull. A ship has a length, an engine and a top speed. A report that needs more than the hull can give did not happen as reported. Our research measures how far ship physics can be trusted between sparse reports.

The crowd. One ship jumping is a fault. Forty ships jumping to one place is a pattern.

A second witness. Radar satellites see ships whether or not they broadcast. Where an image exists, it settles where the ship was.

Questions

What is AIS spoofing?

AIS spoofing is a ship broadcasting a position, identity or movement over the Automatic Identification System that is not real. It happens when the ship's satellite-navigation receiver is fed a false signal, or when the AIS messages themselves are forged or altered.

What is the difference between GNSS jamming and spoofing?

Jamming drowns the satellite signal, so the receiver loses its fix and positions freeze, drift or disappear. Spoofing replaces the signal with a false one, so the receiver confidently reports the wrong place. Mild jamming can look like spoofing in the data.

Can AIS spoofing be detected?

Much of it can be flagged from the data itself: a report that needs a speed no hull can reach, or many ships reporting the same impossible spot. AIS alone rarely shows who did it. Independent sources such as radar satellites can confirm where a ship really was.

Where does AIS spoofing happen?

Interference with ship navigation is reported regularly in the Baltic, the Black Sea, the eastern Mediterranean and the Gulf, and has been recorded off major ports elsewhere, including Los Angeles and Long Beach in January 2026.

What does Anopticon do about it?

Anopticon verifies the positions ships report: each one is checked against what that ship can physically do and against other ships and independent sources, and the finding is published with the data behind it.

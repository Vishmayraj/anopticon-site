Primer

What AIS is, how a ship ends up broadcasting a position it is not in, and how you can tell.

What AIS is

AIS, the Automatic Identification System, is a radio transponder. Every few seconds it broadcasts the ship’s identity, position, course and speed on two VHF channels, 161.975 and 162.025 MHz, that any receiver can hear: other ships, shore stations, satellites, and the websites that turn it all into a map.

Under the SOLAS convention it is carried by ships of 300 gross tonnage and upwards on international voyages, by cargo ships of 500 gross tonnage and upwards, and by all passenger ships. A ship under way sends a position every 2 to 10 seconds; at anchor, every three minutes.

The position comes from the ship’s own satellite-navigation receiver, usually GPS. AIS repeats whatever that receiver believes, and nothing in the message proves who sent it.

Jamming, spoofing, forgery

Jamming
Noise drowns the satellite signal. The receiver loses its fix and positions freeze, drift or vanish. Jamming is cheap and is often a side effect of military activity nearby.
Spoofing
A false satellite signal replaces the real one. The receiver reports the wrong place with full confidence, often many ships at once, often on land.
Forgery
The AIS messages themselves are made up or changed: a borrowed identity, a ship that does not exist, a track that was never sailed, a transponder switched off.

What it looks like

A ship that covers a continent between two reports. Dozens of ships stacked on one point inland. Tracks that loop in perfect circles over a headland. A ship that goes silent for an hour and comes back somewhere it could not have reached.

One ship doing this is usually a faulty receiver. Many ships doing it in the same place at the same time is interference.

How to tell

The hull. A ship has a length, an engine and a top speed. A report that needs more than that did not happen as reported. Our research measures how far ship physics holds between sparse reports.

Other ships. One ship jumping is a fault. Forty jumping to the same place is a pattern, and the pattern often says what kind of interference it was.

A second source. Radar satellites see ships whether or not they broadcast. Where an image exists for the right place and time, it settles where the ship was.

Questions

What is AIS spoofing?

A ship broadcasting a position, identity or movement over the Automatic Identification System that is not real. It happens when the ship's satellite-navigation receiver is fed a false signal, or when the AIS messages themselves are forged.

What is the difference between GNSS jamming and spoofing?

Jamming drowns the satellite signal, so the receiver loses its fix and positions freeze, drift or disappear. Spoofing replaces the signal with a false one, so the receiver reports the wrong place with full confidence. Mild jamming can look like spoofing in the data.

Can AIS spoofing be detected?

A lot of it can be flagged from the data: a report that needs a speed no hull can reach, or many ships reporting the same impossible spot. AIS alone rarely shows who did it. Radar satellites can confirm where a ship really was.

Where does it happen?

Interference is reported regularly in the Baltic, the Black Sea, the eastern Mediterranean and the Gulf, and has been recorded off major ports elsewhere, including Los Angeles and Long Beach in January 2026.

Why does it matter outside navigation?

Because ship positions are evidence. They decide demurrage and laytime, whether a ship entered a sanctioned area, whether a claim for a collision or a grounding holds up, and what a voyage is worth. A false position carries into every one of those.

Ships that were not where they said.

Anopticon tests what machines report against what the physical world allows. We start with ships, which broadcast where they are every few seconds. Here are five times they were wrong.

2013 · 2019 · 2019 · 2026 · 2026

October 2013. Off Italy.

A ship that never existed sailed the word “pwned”.

Researchers at Trend Micro built it with a 700-euro AIS radio and a laptop near a port. Public tracking maps showed it. In the same tests a real tugboat left the Mississippi and turned up on a lake in Dallas.

MIT Technology Review, 18 October 2013

June 2019. Point Reyes, California.

A boat in Nigeria went round in circles over California.

For two weeks from 5 June, a crew boat working an oil terminal in Nigeria broadcast a looping track over Point Reyes. Part of the loop was on land. Bjorn Bergman of SkyTruth found the same thing in eight other places.

The Log, 28 August 2020

July 2019. Shanghai.

Rings on the riverbank.

The container ship Manukai was coming into Shanghai when another ship vanished from its screen and reappeared at a dock. It had been at that dock all along. C4ADS plotted weeks of reports and found ships placed in rings on the east bank of the Huangpu. No one has said who did it.

MIT Technology Review, 15 November 2019

29 January 2026. Los Angeles and Long Beach.

Seven ships jumped at once.

During a published GPS test window, the vessel traffic service saw seven ships jump in reported position across an area more than 100 miles wide, the traffic separation scheme included. One ship’s AIS went quiet for nearly an hour.

Inside GNSS, 2026

10 October 2026. Lima, Peru.

Ships off Istanbul turned up in Peru.

Forty-nine of them, all inside one 3 km patch of inland Lima, 12,221 km from where they were, at a median reported speed of 94.9 knots. This one is ours.
